Travel Sketchbook - February

31/04/2026

From March to June 2026, I watched the seasons change whilst travelling around Japan. Starting in the south on Kyushu island, backpacking all the way up to Mutsu in the North. My dream of travelling Japan began on work trip to Tokyo in 2023, when I saw the sun shining off the rice fields from the Shinkansen on a day trip to Kyoto. I wished to fully immerse myself in their countryside, see the parts outside of the cities, the nature and culture that felt so different to anywhere else I had been. Now I am home, I can try to explain this sensation; safety and calm mixed with mystery and tradition. It's like everyone is born a poet there, there is always the ying to the yang. The hope with the cynicism, the laughter with the grief. I don't speak Japanese well at all, nothing further than a basic conversation and asking for directions, but it seems like the language itself is very poetic. I got this sense particularly from the translations I would see in mueseums, but also in the broken conversations I had with locals along the way. After wishing me strength during a storm or giving me food for my journey, they often shared stories from their town and communities, wanting to share tradition and wisdom, and wanting to hear of Scottish traditions at home. I saw so much incredible Japanese art that shared this mysterious yet historical documentation of Japan. Art is a vital tool that had been used to document change but also communicate change when education was not commonly accessible for the masses. One major example of this in Japanese art is Ukyio-e woodblock prints during the Edo period. This particular artform was essential in documenting life in Japan when it was closed off from the rest of the world for 260 years; what stories were being told, what the cities of Japan looked like, and the fables used to come to terms with natural disasters (yōkai).

I've been a fan of Hokusai, a leading artist in Ukyio-e, for a while now so going to his museum in Tokyo to see some of the hundereds of pieces and books he made on how to draw was a brilliant experience.

Three Kabuki Theaters Thronged for the Eleventh Month Kaomise Performances, Hokusai (1809-13).
Hokusai Manga - Volume 12, Hokusai (1834).
Hida no Takumi Monogatari, Hokusai (1809).

Documenting history in Western civilisations through art had been common practice but at this time it was reserved to contain motifs of those who could pay for it and control its message; religions, royals, governments etc. Ukyio-e woodblock printing was one of the first examples of accessible art that could be cheaply reprinted for the masses and contained subjects of everyday life at the time - farming, fishing, brothels, shopping, construction, protest, disaster. When I looked at more pieces by more modern Japanese artists, I see the same groundedness in their imagination, guided purely through capturing what is around them instead of precribed narratives.

My favourite was "Empancipation of Humanity", Toshi MARUKI (1947), a oil painting filled with texture it felt almost like stain glass looking at it in person.

Empancipation of Humanity", Toshi MARUKI (1947).

The female figure strong and youthful in an eden like garden but it's description grabbing; the artist wished to dipict a hopeful generation of women starting to gain rights and freedom after WW2, but she was unable to capture this hope with the weight of the war still fresh. She then collaborated with Iri Maruki and started painting panels dipicting scenes of the atomic bombs. 15 murals in total which you can see here , a reminder of the complete destruction and pain caused by such a horrific man-made invention. I was fortunate to experience one of the murals "Nagasaki" at the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum as the other 14 where not available to view. Heartbroken doesn't come close to desribing the emotions I felt standing in front of it. The panels record the suffering and pain, the loss, the smoke and fire, the confusion, the innocent. In combination with the museum exhibition, it is a valuable reminder - NEVER AGAIN.

I next went to Teshima Island - one of the art islands set up by contemporary Japanese artists to rejuvinate the economies of islands that no longer have strong industrial ties by using uninhabited houses, old factories or unused land to install contemporary and immersive art. My favourite piece was called La Forêt des Murmures where you walk into a clearing in a forest that has small bells hanging from metal rods with name slips attached to the bells. The visitors are asked to write the name of a loved one who has passed away on the slip and tie it around the bell so that, when the wind blows, you hear the soul of your lost loved one whispering through the forest in a beautiful collective.

The rest of my trip included doing parts of the Kumano Kodo Pilgramage, hiking through the Alps near in Kamikochi, then heading North to Aomori to camp near Lake Towada and Mutsu to see a temple built next to a sulphurous volcano lake. I passed through Matsumoto, the birth place of Yayoi Kusama, and went to her museum where I learnt loads about the artists and the struggle she faced to gain recognition for her distinctive art. In Amori, I went the Nabuta Festival museum and saw the massive, beautiful hand-painted paper floats they make each year. After Mutsu, I travelled to Sendai and Nikko before returning to Tokyo.
