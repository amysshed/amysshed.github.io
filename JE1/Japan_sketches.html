Travel Sketchbook - Japan

31/06/2026

From March to June 2026, I watched the seasons change whilst travelling around Japan . Starting in the south on Kyushu island, backpacking all the way up to Mutsu in the North. My dream of travelling Japan began on work trip to Tokyo in 2023, when I saw the sun shining off the rice fields from the Shinkansen on a day trip to Kyoto. I wanted to fully immerse myself in the Japanese countryside andbsee the parts outside of the cities, the nature and culture that felt so different to anywhere else I had been.

It's a sensation of safety and calm mixed with mystery and tradition. It's like everyone is a poet there, always finding the balance; the hope with the cynicism, the laughter with the grief. I don't speak Japanese well at all, nothing further than a basic conversation and asking for directions, but it seems like the language itself is very poetic. I found this particularly from the translations I would see in mueseums, but also in the broken conversations I had with locals along the way. After wishing me strength during a storm or giving me food for my journey, they often shared stories from their town and communities, wanting to share tradition and wisdom, and wanting to hear of Scottish traditions.

I saw so much incredible Japanese art that shared this mysterious yet historical documentation of Japan. Art is a vital tool that had been used to document change but also communicate change when education was not commonly accessible for the masses. One major example of this in Japanese art is Ukyio-e woodblock prints during the Edo period. This particular artform was essential in documenting life in Japan when it was closed off from the rest of the world for 260 years; what stories were being told, what the cities of Japan looked like, and the fables used to come to terms with natural disasters (yōkai).

I've been a fan of Hokusai, a leading artist in Ukyio-e, for a while now so going to his museum in Tokyo to see some of the hundereds of pieces and books he made on how to draw was a brilliant experience.

Three Kabuki Theaters Thronged for the Eleventh Month Kaomise Performances, Hokusai (1809-13).
Hokusai Manga - Volume 12, Hokusai (1834).
Hida no Takumi Monogatari, Hokusai (1809).

Documenting history in Western civilisations through art had been common practice but at this time it was reserved to contain motifs of those who could pay for it and control its message; religions, royals, governments etc. Ukyio-e woodblock printing was one of the first examples of accessible art that could be cheaply reprinted for the masses and contained subjects of everyday life at the time - farming, fishing, brothels, shopping, construction, protest, disaster. When I looked at pieces by modern Japanese artists, I see the same groundedness in their imagination, guided purely through capturing what is around them instead of precribed narratives.

My favourite was "Empancipation of Humanity", Toshi Maruki (1947), an oil painting filled with texture; it felt almost like stain glass looking at it in person.

Empancipation of Humanity, Toshi MARUKI (1947).

The female figure strong and youthful in an Eden like garden and it's description grabbing; the artist wished to dipict a hopeful generation of women starting to gain rights and freedom after WW2, but she was unable to capture this hope with the weight of the war still fresh. She then collaborated with Iri Maruki and started painting panels dipicting scenes of the atomic bombs. 15 murals in total which you can see here, a reminder of the complete destruction and pain caused by such a horrific man-made invention.

I was fortunate to experience one of the murals "Nagasaki" at the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum as the other 14 where not available to view. Heartbroken doesn't come close to desribing the emotions I felt standing in front of it. The panels record the suffering and pain, the loss, the smoke and fire, the confusion, the innocence. In combination with the museum exhibition, it is a valuable reminder - NEVER AGAIN.

I next went to Teshima Island - one of the art islands set up by contemporary Japanese artists to rejuvinate the economies of islands that no longer have strong industrial ties; using uninhabited houses, old factories or unused land to install contemporary and immersive art.

My favourite piece was called La Forêt des Murmures. Visitors walk into a clearing in a forest that has small bells hanging from metal rods with name slips attached to the bells. The visitors are asked to write the name of a loved one who has passed away on the slip and tie it around the bell so that, when the wind blows, you hear the soul of your lost loved one whispering through the forest in a beautiful collective. It was very moving; a mixture of spiritual and human connection.

The rest of my trip included doing parts of the Kumano Kodo Pilgramage, hiking through the Alps in Kamikochi, then heading North to Aomori, camping by Lake Towada, and Mutsu to see a temple built next to a sulphurous volcano lake.

I passed through Matsumoto, the birth place of Yayoi Kusama, and went to her museum where I learnt about the artist and the struggle she faced to gain recognition for her distinctive art. As the orgininal creator of infinity mirror rooms and thus a leading figure in immersive art, and translating mental health problems into art, she should be known for more than just "the dots artist".

In Amori, I went the Nabuta Festival museum and saw the magnificent hand-painted paper floats they make each year. After Mutsu, I travelled to Sendai and Nikko before returning to Tokyo. Here are some sketches from along this part of the trip.

My time in Japan everything I had hoped for; I met some incredible people, experienced some beautiful stories and artforms, saw such well preserved and respected nature (including so many 800&mdash1000 year old trees!). I was inspired by their mindset on life; gratitude, kindness to all, and the importance of community in their towns. I was able to have time to contemplate what I wanted from life, what I wanted to give, and most essential to me, how I wanted to use my creativity.

My favourite subjects to sketch and take lots of photos of in Japan became texture; tree bark, rust on corregated iron, minerals falling over rock faces. I like these subjects because you can totally immerse yourself in them for a while, feel really connected to your surroundings and the history of the structures. A tree that was planted 800 years ago with thick mosey orange and green bark. A shed built by a grandparent in a garden that no longer heres laughter. A rock that started from granite, now covered in reds and yellows next to the smelly crack in the volcano crater.
