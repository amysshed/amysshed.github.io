Travel Sketchbook - February

31/04/2026

From March to June 2026, I watched the seasons change whilst travelling around Japan. Starting in the south on Kyushu island, backpacking all the way up to Mutsu in the North. My dream of travelling Japan began on work trip to Tokyo in 2023, when I saw the sun shining off the rice fields from the Shinkansen on a day trip to Kyoto. I wished to fully immerse myself in their countryside, see the parts outside of the cities, immerse myself in the nature and culture that felt so different to anywhere else I had been. Now I am home I can try to explain this sensation; safety and calm mixed with mystery and tradition. It's like everyone is born a poet there, there is always the ying to the yang. The hope with the cynicism, the laughter with the grief. I dont speak Japanese well at all, nothing further than a basic conversation and asking for directions, but it seems like the language itself is very poetic. I got this sense particularly from the translations I would see in mueseums, but also in the broken conversations I had with locals along the way. After wishing me strength during a storm or giving me food for my journey, they often shared stories from their town and communities, wanting to share tradition and wisdom, and wanting to hear of Scottish traditions at home. I saw so much incredible Japanese art that shared this mysterious yet historical documentation of Japan. Art is a vital tool that had been used to document change but also communicate change when education was not commonly accessible for the masses. One major example of this in Japanese art is Ukyio-e wood block prints during the Edo period. This particular artform was essential in documenting life in Japan when it was closed off from the rest of the world for 260 years; what stories were being told, what the cities of Japan looked like, and the fables used to come to terms with natural disasters (yōkai). I've been a fan of Hokusai, a leading artist in Ukyio-e, for a while now so going to his museum in Tokyo to see some of the hundereds of pieces and books he made on how to draw was so cool.

Documenting history in Western civilisations through art had been common practice but at this time it was reserved to contain motifs of those who could pay for it and control its message; religions, royals, governments ets. Ukyio-e woodblock printing is one of the first examples of accessible art that can be cheaply reprinted for the masses and contained subjects of everyday life at the time - farming, fishing, brothels, shopping, construction, protest, disaster. When I looked at more pieces by more modern Japanese art, I see the same groundedness in their imagination, guided purely through capturing what is around them instead of precribed narratives. In the MOMAT (The National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo) I saw some incredible paintings capturing Japanese life. PHOTOS OF PAINTINGS FROM MOMAT My favourite was "Empancipation of Humanity", Toshi MARUKI (1947), a oil painting filled with texture it felt almost like stain glass looking at it in person. The female figure strong and youthful in an eden like garden but it's description grabbing; the artist wished to dipict a hopeful generation of women starting to gain rights and freedom after WW2, but she was unable to capture this hope with the weight of the war still fresh. She then collaborated with Iri Maruki and started painting panels dipicting scenes of the atomic bombs. 15 murals in total which you can see here INSERT LINK https://marukigallery.jp/en/hiroshimapanels/, a reminder of the complete destruction and pain caused by such a horrific man-made invention. I was fortunate to experience one of the murals "Nagasaki" at the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum as the other 14 where not available to view. Heartbroken doesn't come close to desribing the emotions I felt standing in front of it. The panels record the suffering and pain, the loss, the smoke and fire, the confusion, the innocent. In combination with the museum exhibition, it is a valuable reminder - NEVER AGAIN. Here are some sketches I created travelling through Shikoku and Shodoshima A brilliant evolution of Japanese art is found on Teshima island - one of the art islands set up by contemporary japanese artists to rejuvinate the economies of islands that no longer have strong industrial ties by using uninhabited houses, old factories or unused land to install contempory and immersive art. My favourite piece was called --- where you walk into a clearing in the forrest that has small bells hanging form metal rods and have name slips attached to the bells. The visitors are asked to write the name of a loved one who has passed away on the slip and tie it around the bell so that, when the wind blows, you hear the soul of your lost loved one whispering through the forrest, in a collective with the souls of the loved ones of the other visitors to this space. Here there was that common thread of tradition and human emotion, reminding the vistitors how similar we all are. Peru

Peru was an incredible experience and a place I hope to go back to one day. Their love of tradition and craft is so inspiring and, when you speak to locals, you can feel their pride in the ancestors for the sacrifices they made in protecting their traditions during colonoisation by the Spanish. It's a wonder of a country with so much natural beauty. I cannot wait to explore there again one day. My time in Lima was short but I went to the Museum of Pre-Columbian textiles, displaying original textiles and crafts from the Inca and Quechuan era and the complex methods they used to create vibrant and intuitive textiles for their communities.

The Contemporary Art Museum of Lima also had a brilliant collection of artworks centered in Peru, including the Francesco Mariotti & María Luy joint exhibit (read more here), which included a very powerful installation where Luy made 8 postcards, each with a drawing that her daughters depicting scenes of the Lebanon war through childrens eyes, then on the reverse of the card, Luy added images from news articles documenting Palestinian refugees being killed during the Sabra and Shatila Massacre in 1982. Luy added quotes from major artists about the revolutionary power of art alongside the images. The distressing images of the Palestinan victims, including children, being so closely presented with the innocent childish depicitions of war was gut-wrentching. A clear reminder of the inncocence of the victims of war and genocide, right there as plain as day on a A3 piece of card.
