La Boca

05/04/2026

It’s been almost two months since I spent half a day in La Boca, and I’m still thinking about it. My friends and I went on a free walking tour of the area in Buenos Aires, and I fell in love. The colourful buildings painted with left-over ship paint, the art on every corner, and the strong sense of community made my creative soul swoon.

We learnt about Quinquela, the man who gained the title “The painter of La Boca”—an inspiring figure who captured his community in grand oil paintings and, after gaining success, ensured that his community would have access to the art world by asking artists to create permanent pieces of art on the streets of La Boca for all to see. He also opened a museum for the community to access Argentinian art, alongside founding schools and healthcare facilities.

Standing in Quinquela’s old flat on the top floor of the museum, looking at the thickly painted canvases, I thought of the paintings my dad loves that I grew up with, depicting images of Glasgow from when he was young, with busy shipyards and their strong workers. These paintings typically use dark colour palettes and heavy outlines to capture the harsh environment and transport you to a rough and unforgiving time in Scotland. Quinquela captures a similar subject matter of busy shipyards, but instead uses a vibrant colour palette and creates sun-soaked, energetic images by loading thick slabs of oil paint onto a palette knife and swirling the colours together.

Maybe it only highlights the difference in the weather between the cities but standing in the museum and seeing Quinquellas deeply engrained relationship to his community, I believe it captures the communities these artists were working within; one filled with economic promise and another plaged with abandonment. 

Within the museum, there was a collection of amazing pieces by other Argentinian artists. My favourite piece of the day had to be Plena tarea by Mario Baima (1975). I was immediately drawn into the piece by the brushstrokes, which create a wash of reflections not only on the sea but throughout the whole work. The images I have really do not do it justice, but here’s a link to the piece on the museum website: [ Mario Baima. Plena tarea, 1975]. He captures the smog and industrial materials of the dock and soaks them in sunlight, bouncing around the piece and creating incredible reflections. When looking at the brushstrokes on the canvas, particularly the crane structure on the right, you realise how purposeful every mark is. Using a square brush loaded with an off-white, he carves out the steel structure. All that, and I’ve not even touched on the moody colour palette.

La Boca, I love you. I felt at home whilst walking through your streets.
