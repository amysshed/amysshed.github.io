La Boca

05/04/2026

It’s been almost two months since I spent half a day in La Boca and it's my new "Roman Empire". My friends and I went on a free walking tour of the area in Buenos Aires, and I fell in love. The colourful buildings painted with left-over ship paint, the art on every corner, and the strong sense of community made my creative soul swoon.

We learnt about Quinquela, the man who gained the title “The painter of La Boca”— an inspiring figure who documented his humble community in grand oil paintings and, after gaining success, ensured that his community would have access to the art world by asking artists to create permanent pieces of art on the streets of La Boca for all to see. He also opened a museum for the community to access Argentinian art, alongside founding schools and healthcare facilities.

Standing in Quinquela’s old flat on the top floor of the museum, I thought of the paintings my dad loves depicting Glasgow shipyards, filled with busy and strong workers. These paintings typically use dark colour palettes and heavy outlines to capture the harsh environment and transport you to a rough and unforgiving time in Scotland. Quinquela captures a similar subject matter of busy shipyards, but instead uses a vibrant colour palette and creates sun-soaked, energetic images by loading thick slabs of oil paint onto a palette knife and swirling the colours together.

The Heroic Dosser, 1984. Howson.
Chimeness, 1930. Quinquella.

This was a reminder of the harsh weather of Glasgow compared to Buenos Aires, but also highlighted the differences in zeitgeists between the yards in the different times in history. One filled with economic promise and another scarred with abandonment.

Quinquella Pallet Strokes. Descarga del Horno, 1932.

The museum featured a collection of amazing pieces by other Argentinian artists. My favourite piece of the day had to be Plena tarea by Mario Baima (1975). I was immediately drawn into the piece by the brushstrokes which created a wash of reflections not only on the sea but throughout the whole work. The images I have really do not do it justice, but here’s a link to the piece on the museum website: [ Mario Baima. Plena tarea, 1975]. He captures the smog and industrial materials of the dock and soaks them in sunlight, bouncing around the piece and creating incredible reflections. When looking at the brushstrokes on the canvas, particularly the crane structure on the right, you realise how purposeful every mark is. Using a square brush loaded with an off-white, he carves out the steel structure. All that, and I’ve not even touched on the moody colour palette.

Plena tarea, 1975. Mario Baima.
